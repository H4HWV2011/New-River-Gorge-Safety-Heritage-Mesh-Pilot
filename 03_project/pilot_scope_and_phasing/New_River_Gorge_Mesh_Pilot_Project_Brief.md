# New River Gorge Heritage & Visitor Connectivity Mesh Pilot

**A Harmony for Hope Community Initiative**  
**Program Sponsor:** Harmony for Hope, Inc. (501(c)(3) public charity)  
**Intelligence Platform (ALLIS) Developer/Operator:** Kidd's Technical Services  
**Service Area:** New River Gorge National Park & Preserve and gateway communities  
**First-Phase Sites:** Mount Hope · Thurmond  
**Potential Future Sites:** Fayetteville, Oak Hill, Hinton, Ansted, Hawks Nest

---

> **Project status: Funding readiness, with two committed first-phase sites.** No ALLIS node or public Wi‑Fi mesh is deployed in the corridor yet. The first funded phase would establish two anchor sites: the Fayette County Community Arts Center in Mount Hope (site controlled by Harmony for Hope) and Thurmond, which has written endorsement from both the Town of Thurmond and the New River Gorge National Park and Preserve superintendent. For Thurmond, an NPS-managed site, the National Park Service compliance process — special-use permitting and Section 106 review — follows grant funding and precedes any installation. Fayetteville and other gateway communities are potential future sites, each contingent on separate permissions, funding, and review. Corridor-scale language describes the intended future model, not a current deployment.

## A Corridor of Stories, Not Just a Park

The New River Gorge is more than a set of overlooks and whitewater put‑ins. It is a 53‑mile corridor of coal camps, rail towns, and main streets that once powered the industrial United States and now anchor some of West Virginia's fastest‑growing tourism. Visitors arrive to hike, climb, and raft one of the oldest rivers in the world, and they move through gateway towns like Fayetteville, Oak Hill, Hinton, Ansted, Mount Hope, and rail hamlets like Thurmond along the way.

State and local tourism partners describe these communities as "must‑add" destinations for anyone visiting the Gorge — places to eat, stay, shop, and explore turn‑of‑the‑century depots and coal‑era architecture. The National Park Service highlights preserved railroad towns like Thurmond as links to a past that remains largely untouched by modern development. Harmony for Hope and its partners have built heritage infrastructure in Mount Hope that invites visitors to walk a 1.25‑mile trail through sixteen points of architectural significance and coal‑company history.

The scale of visitation gives this work real economic weight. In a June 2026 letter of support for this project, the New River Gorge National Park and Preserve superintendent noted that in 2024 the park contributed $94 million to the local gateway communities outside its boundaries. Yet in many of these places, visitors arrive with almost no connectivity. Their phones show a bar or two of service, if any. They can't reliably load trail maps or safety information, and they can't easily reach the deep interpretive work that local organizations and NPS have already created. In a corridor where numerous former coal towns and multiple historic districts are accessible from roads and trails, much of the story never makes it into the field.

The New River Gorge Heritage & Visitor Connectivity Mesh Pilot is proposed to help change that — beginning with two committed community sites and, if proven, extending along the corridor in partnership with the communities that live here, without compromising the character of the places visitors come to see.

## What the Mesh Is Intended to Do

The **Artificial Learning & Location Intelligence System (ALLIS)** is a place‑based intelligence platform developed by Kidd's Technical Services. It currently has a public online presence at egeria.mountainshares.us. The locally hosted, mesh‑node architecture described below is the proposed design for this pilot — a public Wi‑Fi mesh and a network of community nodes are not currently deployed in the corridor.

Under the proposed design, each node would be designated **MS.___.ALLIS** and function as a small, self‑contained local system: storing maps, heritage stories, trail data, safety guidance, and AI‑assisted visitor support on‑site, in the community it serves.

When the pilot is built and a visitor connects to an ALLIS node, the intended experience is that they would:

- Connect to local intelligence rather than a distant cloud server — a system oriented to the depot they are standing in, the trail they are about to hike, or the river reach they are looking at.
- Reach heritage content created by NPS and local organizations, including when upstream internet is intermittent or overloaded.
- Receive context‑specific safety information suited to the landscape and to the realities of rural emergency response.

The `MS.` prefix in each proposed node name signals that the deployment would operate under the **MountainShares Sponsorship Tier**, an intended community‑benefit model operated by Harmony for Hope, Inc. Under this model, host communities and agency partners would not pay installation or subscription fees; those costs would be pursued by Harmony for Hope through grant development, as detailed in the project budget. Kidd's Technical Services would own and operate ALLIS as an independent platform; Harmony for Hope, through MountainShares, would sponsor the no‑cost host tier; and each host partner would enter a simple governance agreement with Kidd's Technical Services to host a node and help shape its content.

MountainShares is a developing community‑participation and value‑recognition framework. It is not represented here as an available grant match, an independently funded operating entity, or a current public Wi‑Fi operator. Its role in this pilot is the intended sponsorship structure described above, to be activated as funding and agreements permit.

## Why Gateway Towns Are the Focus

When New River Gorge was elevated to national park and preserve status, it accelerated tourism growth across southern West Virginia. Towns like Fayetteville and Oak Hill became adventure hubs, with outfitters, resorts, and a concentration of visitor‑facing businesses. Hinton, at the southern rail end, remains defined by its historic depot and downtown district tied to the long‑distance rail line. Ansted and Hawks Nest anchor the northern rim at the junction of the Gorge and the Midland Trail.

These gateway towns bear much of the pressure of park visitation — traffic, housing, and emergency calls — and they carry the responsibility of welcoming visitors into the region's story. They are also where connectivity gaps are most visible and most consequential. A visitor trying to find a trailhead in Mount Hope, a family arriving by train in Thurmond, a climber in Fayetteville checking river levels: all are interacting with the park through the lens of these communities.

Local, heritage‑aware connectivity in these towns is proposed not as an add‑on to park infrastructure, but as part of making tourism work for the people who live here — so that when the state invites the world to experience the New River Gorge from its gateway towns, those experiences are supported by infrastructure that reflects where visitors actually are and helps keep them safer while they are here.

## Two First-Phase Sites: Mount Hope and Thurmond

The first funded phase would establish two anchor sites that sit in very different places along the corridor — a living coalfield main street and a preserved rail boomtown — bound together by coal, rail, and the river they overlook.

### Mount Hope — A Living Coalfield Main Street (First Anchor)

Mount Hope is not a tourist postcard town. It is a coalfield community that rebuilt itself in brick, stone, and concrete after the Fire of 1910 and earned the name "Phoenix City of the New River Gorge." Harmony for Hope and local partners established the Mount Hope Historic Walking Trail — a 1.25‑mile route with sixteen points of architectural significance tracing the town's evolution from early pathways to company town to modern coalfield municipality.

On one short section of Main Street, the density of community institutions is notable:

- Harmony for Hope, which operates from the 704–708 Main Street buildings (mailing address 706 Main Street), a hub for arts and youth programming.
- The trailhead kiosk for the Historic Walking Trail, on the bank parking lot one door north, where visitors already encounter brochures and QR‑linked interpretation.
- The Fayette County Community Arts Center, which anchors cultural programming for the wider region and is the proposed first public‑access site.

In Mount Hope, a first ALLIS node is proposed to do three things at once:

1. **Support an existing trail.** A node designated MS.MHHWT.ALLIS would extend the Historic Walking Trail into a living digital layer. Visitors picking up a brochure at the kiosk could connect to locally hosted content that deepens the stories the printed panels introduce — where businesses and company offices stood, and how residents rebuilt after the fire, in the words and voice the community chooses.

2. **Serve a working main street.** The same node could deliver connectivity and safety information for everyday residents: weather alerts, road closures, heat advisories, and information about events at the arts center. Mount Hope is first a place where people live, not a set piece for visitors.

3. **Demonstrate the model where the organization already works.** Because Harmony for Hope operates from 704–708 Main Street, backbone hardware could be installed at 704 Main Street under the existing Harmony for Hope–Kidd's Technical Services relationship, subject to site and technical review. A relay on the building and a small node at the trailhead kiosk would complete the initial mesh, without needing to negotiate with large agencies for the first deployment.

Mount Hope, in this pilot, is the reference implementation — the place to learn what it takes to make ALLIS work in a coalfield main‑street context and how to share stewardship with a municipal government and a local arts institution.

### Thurmond — A Preserved Rail Boomtown (Committed First-Phase Site)

Thurmond, by contrast, is nearly empty of residents and full of story. Once one of the busiest revenue points on the Chesapeake & Ohio Railway, it is now functionally a preserved outdoor museum. NPS restored the Thurmond Depot as a visitor center and maintains other key structures in the townsite; only a handful of residents remain.

Visitors arrive by road, by long‑distance rail, and by special excursion trains that use the Depot as a staging point, walking through a place whose mission is to remain a link to the industrial past and to tell the story of the coal and rail economy that once dominated the valley.

Thurmond is a committed first-phase site with written support from both the community and the federal site authority. The Town of Thurmond, through Mayor Melanie Dragan, has provided a letter of support endorsing Thurmond's role as a node in the corridor pilot, subject to applicable National Park Service permitting, Section 106 review, and federal governance processes. The New River Gorge National Park and Preserve, through Acting Superintendent Bryan Wender, has provided a letter of support for the project's grant proposals and its potential to enhance interpretation of the cultural heritage in the Thurmond area, noting that the NPS compliance process would be completed after grant funding and before proceeding, to ensure the final product aligns with the historic setting and minimizes impact on natural and cultural resources.

A node at Thurmond would:

1. **Deepen interpretation where connectivity is absent.** A node designated MS.THURMOND.ALLIS would give visitors a locally hosted interpretive layer that works even without cell service — the railroad story, the company‑town story, the labor story, and the environmental story, in formats that complement ranger‑led walks and existing panels.

2. **Connect heritage to safety.** The same system could deliver river‑safety information, trail advisories, and emergency guidance tailored to the immediate context.

3. **Respect federal governance.** As an NPS-managed site, Thurmond deployment would proceed through the special-use permitting and Section 106 review the superintendent's letter identifies, following grant funding. A federal carve‑out in the governance framework confirms that partner participation is not a procurement, does not obligate federal funds, and does not override federal records or privacy law.

With written endorsements from both the town and the park superintendent already in hand, Thurmond's remaining path is the NPS compliance process — the review that turns an endorsed site into a cleared one once funding is secured.

## How the Program Is Intended to Work

### Proposed Ownership and Sponsorship

- **ALLIS** would be owned and operated by Kidd's Technical Services as an independent platform.
- **MountainShares**, operated by Harmony for Hope, Inc., would sponsor the MS.___ host tier as a community benefit, with pilot costs pursued through grant development.
- **Community partners** — municipalities, nonprofits, and agencies such as NPS — would host nodes, contribute content, and help define priorities.

### Agreements and Responsibilities

Each node would be governed by a simple, written Corridor Partner Community Governance Agreement between Kidd's Technical Services and the host organization. In plain terms:

- The partner's contribution is in‑kind: site access, a point of contact, and participation in evaluation.
- Node hardware would be installed and maintained at no cost to the host partner under the proposed sponsorship model.
- Locally generated operational and heritage data would be treated as a community resource, and partner‑contributed content would remain the partner's intellectual property.
- A partner could terminate participation with notice, triggering removal of hardware and restoration of surfaces.

For federal partners, a Federal Agency Participation Carve‑Out would confirm that the agreement is not a procurement, does not obligate federal funds, and does not override federal records or privacy law.

## From Two Anchors to a Possible Corridor

Mount Hope and Thurmond are the two first-phase nodes in what could, if the model proves out, become a corridor‑scale mesh serving the New River Gorge and its gateway towns. Any such expansion is contingent on demonstrated benefit, funding, site permissions, partner coordination, technical review, and operational capacity.

Candidate future communities include:

- **Fayetteville** — the high‑visibility adventure gateway at the rim.
- **Oak Hill** — the resort and rafting hub serving overnight guests and day‑trippers.
- **Hinton** — the southern rail end with its depot and historic downtown district.
- **Ansted and Hawks Nest** — northern‑rim communities tied to the Midland Trail and older travel routes.

Each town has its own story. ALLIS is not intended to overwrite those stories with a single script; it is intended to provide a scaffold for communities, NPS, and program partners to tell them more fully, in more places — and to help keep residents and visitors safer while doing so.

## What Success Looks Like for the First Phase

The pilot's first phase would be successful if, at its conclusion, three things are true:

1. **Visitors understand more of where they are.** A family walking the Historic Trail in Mount Hope, or standing on the platform in Thurmond, leaves with a deeper sense of coal, rail, labor, and landscape than signage alone provides.

2. **Communities feel more in control, not less.** Municipal and nonprofit partners in Mount Hope, and NPS staff at Thurmond, experience the infrastructure as serving their priorities rather than dictating them.

3. **The model can travel.** When Fayetteville, Oak Hill, Hinton, or Ansted later consider participation, they can be invited to review a documented, evaluated two-site pilot they can visit and see — not an abstraction.

## How to Engage

This is an invitation to collaborate on a proposed pilot with two committed first-phase sites, not a pitch for a finished product. Gateway communities, NPS staff, tourism partners, and local organizations who see a place for their town or site are encouraged to start with a conversation and a walk:

- Walk Main Street and the Historic Trail in Mount Hope with Harmony for Hope.
- Walk the Depot platform and grade crossing in Thurmond with NPS interpretive staff.
- Walk Fayetteville's courthouse square, Oak Hill's outfitter row, or Hinton's depot district, and ask what a locally hosted, heritage‑aware mesh could do there that today's infrastructure does not.

From there, the path for any site is straightforward and sequential: identify a site host, complete a site survey and technical review, secure funding and any required permissions, execute the governance agreement, and — only then — stand up a node.

The New River Gorge has always been a corridor of stories. This pilot is a proposal to give those stories a way to travel the same routes visitors already take — from trailhead to depot to main street — while keeping them in the hands of the communities that own them.
