# Peer Community-Owned Networks and Digital Sovereignty Case Studies

## Purpose

This document identifies peer precedents for the proposed **New River Gorge Safety & Heritage Mesh Pilot**. The examples below demonstrate approaches to community-owned or community-governed connectivity, local digital stewardship, public-interest information access, and resilient communications.

They are included as planning precedents—not as claims that Harmony for Hope’s proposed pilot has achieved the same scale, technical capability, funding base, service model, legal authority, or disaster-resilience outcome.

The New River Gorge Safety & Heritage Mesh Pilot remains a phased proposal. Any future deployment will require site permissions, technical validation, funding, governance, privacy and security planning, maintenance capacity, and appropriate community and partner agreements.

## Peer Profiles

### Guifi.net — Catalonia, Spain

**Model:** Guifi.net is a long-running community network associated with a commons-based model of shared infrastructure. It began in rural Catalonia and expanded through participation by residents, businesses, and public institutions.

**What the precedent demonstrates:**

- Community-built network infrastructure can grow beyond a single initial site.
- Shared rules and open participation can support infrastructure as a community resource.
- Rural and underserved communities can organize connectivity solutions rather than waiting exclusively for conventional providers.

Published research described Guifi.net as the world’s largest community network by node count and coverage area, reporting more than 27,000 operational nodes in 2015; later research described more than 30,000 nodes. Current scale figures should be confirmed directly with Guifi.net before use in funding applications.  

**Relevance to Harmony for Hope:** Guifi.net supports the principle that a modest local deployment can be designed as a community asset with governance, maintenance, and shared-benefit structures from the beginning. It is a governance and commons precedent, not a like-for-like technical model for the proposed Mount Hope pilot.

### NYC Mesh — New York City, United States

**Model:** NYC Mesh is a community-operated network that uses rooftop wireless links, nodes, and higher-capacity “supernodes” to connect participants and provide paths to the wider internet.

**What the precedent demonstrates:**

- Community networks can use a mix of local rooftop nodes and higher-capacity gateway locations.
- Technical documentation, member participation, and repeatable installation practices can make a network more understandable and maintainable.
- Network growth can occur incrementally as new locations are connected.

NYC Mesh describes a node as a location with one or more routers and a supernode as a site with multiple routers, potentially servers, and a fast gateway connection to the broader internet.  

**Relevance to Harmony for Hope:** NYC Mesh offers a useful precedent for phased deployment, documentation, local installation practices, and distinguishing local nodes from backhaul or gateway infrastructure. Mount Hope’s first phase would be far smaller and must be designed for its own terrain, host-site conditions, power, backhaul, permissions, and public-interest uses.

### Red Hook WiFi — Brooklyn, New York

**Model:** Red Hook WiFi was developed by the Red Hook Initiative with Open Technology Institute support as a locally owned community wireless network. It combined neighborhood infrastructure with a Digital Stewards model that trained local young adults in network construction, maintenance, and community engagement.

**What the precedent demonstrates:**

- Community training can pair technical stewardship with community-organizing skills.
- A local network can support public information, device charging, volunteer coordination, and communication during disruption.
- Resilience depends on more than routers: power, upstream connectivity, local staffing, physical locations, and trusted community institutions all matter.

During Hurricane Sandy, local cell service was unavailable in Red Hook. The Red Hook Initiative retained power and a Verizon connection, allowing its local WiFi network to continue providing access. The organization became a recovery hub, and FEMA later supplied a satellite uplink that expanded the available connection.  

**Relevance to Harmony for Hope:** Red Hook WiFi is a strong precedent for planning public-interest information, community stewardship, and emergency-adjacent usefulness. It does not establish that the proposed Mount Hope pilot will operate during outages; that outcome would depend on future power, backhaul, equipment, operations, and emergency-coordination planning.

### Equitable Internet Initiative — Detroit, Michigan

**Model:** The Equitable Internet Initiative, associated with the Detroit Community Technology Project and neighborhood anchor organizations, develops neighborhood-governed wireless networks while training residents as Digital Stewards.

**What the precedent demonstrates:**

- Local residents can be trained in community organizing and wireless-network skills.
- Community anchors can support network deployment, outreach, governance, and maintenance.
- Digital-equity work can connect connectivity with workforce development, local decision-making, and community wealth.

The Equitable Internet Initiative describes its work as neighborhood-governed community wireless networks and reports that it trained 45 Digital Stewards across three Detroit neighborhoods in 2017 through a 20-week program combining community organizing and wireless engineering.  

**Relevance to Harmony for Hope:** EII is a useful precedent for a future Community Evaluation Cohort or local stewardship model. It supports the idea that community members can participate in evaluating, explaining, maintaining, and shaping public-interest digital infrastructure. Any paid cohort, compensation structure, or MountainShares-related model would require its own funding, governance, labor, tax, privacy, and program-design review.

## Themes for the Pilot

### Community stewardship is core infrastructure

Across these precedents, the network is not only equipment. It depends on trusted local people, host institutions, clear responsibilities, public communication, maintenance practices, and an understandable purpose.

### Start with a modest, useful deployment

Peer networks show the value of beginning with a defined site, practical community use, manageable equipment, and clear documentation. The proposed Mount Hope pilot should begin with a limited first phase rather than claim corridor-wide coverage.

### Build local technical capacity

Digital Stewards and similar models demonstrate that technical systems can be paired with local training, community education, paid work where funding permits, and transparent operational roles.

### Treat information as a public-interest resource

A connectivity layer is more useful when paired with locally relevant content: heritage interpretation, visitor information, events, services, public notices, local resources, and community-maintained knowledge.

### Design resilience carefully

Community networks may support useful local communication during disruptions, but resilience is not automatic. It requires explicit planning for power, backhaul, hardware, weather, backups, staffing, maintenance, security, and public communications.

### Use governance proportional to the project

Commons-oriented models show the importance of clear rules for access, stewardship, data, equipment, maintenance, decision-making, and responsible expansion. Governance should be appropriate to the scale and phase of the pilot.

## Application to Mount Hope

Harmony for Hope has already demonstrated place-based public-information implementation through the existing Mount Hope Historic Walking Trail, including installed interpretive signs, printed brochures, a kiosk, QR-enabled Clio access, local-history interpretation, and public-facing heritage resources.

The proposed New River Gorge Safety & Heritage Mesh Pilot would be a separate next-stage initiative. It would explore whether a limited, locally stewarded connectivity and information-access layer could complement that existing public heritage infrastructure.

Potential future applications may include:

- Access to heritage interpretation and visitor resources
- Locally maintained public-interest information
- Community events, programs, and service information
- Public wayfinding and place-based resources
- Local stewardship and evaluation opportunities
- Carefully designed resilient-information functions, subject to technical and operational validation

## Source Notes

- Guifi.net scale figures vary by date and source. Confirm current figures directly with Guifi.net before citing them as current.
- NYC Mesh node counts change as the network evolves. Use its current network map or official reporting for any numerical claim.
- Red Hook WiFi demonstrates the value of local network capacity during Hurricane Sandy, but its continued operation depended on available local power, upstream connectivity, staff, and later FEMA satellite support.
- Detroit’s Digital Stewards model is a precedent for community training and participation. It is not a plug-and-play staffing model; any local adaptation requires its own funding, training, employment, governance, and safety plan.
