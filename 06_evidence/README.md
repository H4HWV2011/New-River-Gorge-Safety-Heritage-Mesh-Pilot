# Grant Evidence

This directory contains grant-related evidence, planning records, program-development materials, submitted applications, correspondence, budgets, and other funding-development records connected to the **New River Gorge Safety & Heritage Mesh Pilot** and related Harmony for Hope program development.

The purpose of this directory is to preserve a clear record of how potential funding opportunities move from early ideas into developed programs, applications, funder review, and — where applicable — implementation.

Grant activity is part of the project's evidence record.

A funding opportunity, planning document, support letter, grant concept, working budget, or submitted proposal does **not** by itself establish that a project component is funded, authorized, approved, purchased, installed, or operational.

---

## Evidence Principle

The grant archive distinguishes among:

```text
funding idea
    ↓
grant concept
    ↓
program / application development
    ↓
submitted application
    ↓
funder review
    ↓
award / decline / revision
    ↓
accepted award and approved scope
    ↓
implementation
    ↓
evaluation
    ↓
closeout and reporting
```

Only the stage actually supported by the record should be claimed.

For example:

```text
concept
    ≠
application

application
    ≠
submission

submission
    ≠
award

award
    ≠
every requested activity approved

approved scope
    ≠
implementation

implementation
    ≠
successful outcome
```

This distinction allows Harmony for Hope to preserve an ambitious development record without confusing future possibilities with current authority or completed work.

# 1. Grant Concepts

* [Grant Concepts](concepts/README.md)

Current concept materials include:

* [Community Champion Transferability](concepts/Community%20Champion%20Transferability.md)
* [Community Connectivity & Community Champion Demonstration](concepts/Community%20Connectivity%20%26%20Community%20Champion%20Demonstration.md)

These documents explore how existing Harmony for Hope and MountainShares-related program ideas might translate into fundable public-benefit projects.

A concept may explore:

* a possible funding opportunity;
* a target population;
* a proposed implementation model;
* transferability to another site;
* an estimated cost;
* a community-engagement structure;
* a training model;
* a potential funder fit; or
* a program idea that may later support one or more applications.

Concept documents are deliberately maintained separately from submitted applications.

Their presence means the idea has been developed enough to document and evaluate.

It does **not** mean:

* a grant application has been submitted;
* a funder has reviewed the idea;
* a grant has been awarded;
* the proposed budget has been approved; or
* implementation has begun.

## Community Champion Development Layer

The Community Champion materials are particularly important because they define the **human and community-stewardship layer** between technical infrastructure and real-world public use.

They help answer questions such as:

* Who helps introduce a new community system?
* Who helps residents understand it?
* How is community feedback gathered?
* How are observations documented?
* What authority does a participant have — and what authority do they not have?
* How can local knowledge inform a governed system without automatically becoming verified fact?
* How can a model developed in Mount Hope be evaluated before transfer to another community?

These materials are program-development documents.

They do not automatically establish:

* employment;
* participant selection;
* public launch;
* funding;
* certification;
* administrative authority;
* system permissions; or
* implementation.

Those conditions must be established separately where applicable.

---

## Development Is Not Submission

A document may be highly developed and still remain a development record.

For example, a detailed framework may include:

* objectives;
* roles;
* schedules;
* compensation assumptions;
* training modules;
* field procedures;
* evaluation standards; and
* estimated costs.

That level of detail does not itself turn the document into a submitted grant request.

The repository therefore preserves the distinction:

```text
program-development maturity
        ≠
application submission
```

This is especially important for reusable frameworks that may eventually support multiple grants or implementation sites.

## Why Submitted Applications Are Preserved

Submitted applications are retained as historical evidence of what Harmony for Hope actually represented to a funder at a particular point in time.

They may document:

* the requested project scope;
* the budget used at submission;
* the proposed site sequence;
* the partners identified at the time;
* the project's maturity at submission;
* the funding amount requested;
* implementation assumptions;
* program language; and
* the evolution of the broader project.

Historical applications should generally remain preserved as submitted rather than being rewritten every time the current strategy changes.

That allows the repository to maintain provenance.

---

## Historical Application ≠ Current Project Scope

A submitted application may contain language that differs from the project's current planning documents.

For example, an earlier application may reflect:

* a prior budget;
* an earlier site sequence;
* an earlier implementation assumption;
* a different funding strategy;
* older project terminology; or
* a narrower or broader proposed scope.

That does not necessarily make the historical record inaccurate.

It means the document reflects the project at the time of submission.

# Award and Scope Discipline

If Harmony for Hope receives a future award, the repository should distinguish carefully between:

```text
application request
        ↓
award notice
        ↓
accepted award
        ↓
approved scope
        ↓
allowable costs
        ↓
implementation
```

A funder may award:

* less than the amount requested;
* only part of a proposed scope;
* funding with restrictions;
* funding contingent on approvals;
* a planning grant rather than an implementation grant; or
* funding subject to match, procurement, reporting, or other requirements.

An award should therefore be documented according to the actual award terms rather than assumed to authorize everything described in the original application.

---

# Declined and Unfunded Applications

Declined applications remain useful evidence.

A declined application may document:

* project development;
* funder fit;
* prior budget assumptions;
* reviewer feedback;
* the timing of project evolution;
* unsuccessful funding pathways; and
* lessons that informed later requests.

A declined application should not be removed merely because funding was not awarded.

Where the status is known and appropriate for public release, the archive should distinguish the outcome clearly.

---

# Revision and Resubmission

Grant development is iterative.

An application may be:

* revised;
* narrowed;
* expanded;
* resubmitted;
* adapted to a new funder;
* separated into multiple funding requests; or
* incorporated into a later implementation plan.

When this occurs, the repository should preserve enough provenance to distinguish the earlier record from the later version.

The goal is not to create one permanently polished historical narrative.

The goal is to maintain a traceable project-development record.

---

# Relationship to Program Development

Not every document in the grant archive exists only to secure a grant.

Some development materials may become standing program frameworks.

For example, Community Champion documents can function as:

* grant-support materials;
* implementation frameworks;
* training architecture;
* field-governance guidance;
* evaluation structures; or
* templates for adaptation in other communities.

Their location in the grant-development archive reflects where and why they were developed.

It does not necessarily limit their later programmatic use.

---

# Community Champion and Testing Cohorts Are Distinct

The repository currently contains more than one human-participation model.

The **Community Champion development materials** describe community stewardship, training, local participation, field observation, and program implementation.

The **six-month application and community testing cohort** included in the current first-year pilot budget serves a different function: structured QA, browser, application, accessibility, and community-use testing.

These models may complement one another, but they should not automatically be treated as the same cohort, the same budget, or the same funded activity.

Where a future grant combines or coordinates them, that relationship should be made explicit in the applicable funding and implementation documents.

# Grant Record Classification

For reviewer clarity:

| Record type           | Meaning                                                       |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Funding idea          | Possible opportunity or source                                |
| Grant concept         | Early structured funding idea                                 |
| Development document  | Program, budget, or implementation work under development     |
| Draft application     | Application being prepared but not submitted                  |
| Submitted application | Request actually delivered to a funder                        |
| Award notice          | Evidence that funding was awarded                             |
| Accepted award        | Organization has accepted funding subject to applicable terms |
| Approved scope        | Activities actually authorized by the award                   |
| Implementation record | Evidence approved work occurred                               |
| Evaluation            | Evidence of observed results                                  |
| Closeout              | Final grant reporting and completion record                   |

A file should be classified according to what it actually represents, not according to what the broader project hopes to become.

---

# Evidence and Provenance Standard

Where practical, grant records should preserve:

* funder name;
* program name;
* submission date;
* funding cycle;
* requested amount;
* project title;
* submitted scope;
* applicant;
* collaborators named in the application;
* application status;
* award status;
* relevant correspondence;
* associated budget;
* revision history; and
* source provenance.

Historical filenames may be retained where changing them would weaken provenance or make it harder to connect a record to the original submission.

---

# Public-Release Standard

Grant records are reviewed before public release for:

* privacy;
* personal information;
* confidential correspondence;
* account or portal information;
* signatures;
* bank or financial-account details;
* protected contact information;
* third-party copyright;
* contractual restrictions;
* funder confidentiality requirements; and
* other sensitive information.

A complete internal grant file may therefore contain records that are not appropriate for publication in the public repository.

Public transparency does not require publication of credentials, protected financial information, private correspondence, or other restricted records.

# Grant Evidence Stewardship Principle

The purpose of this archive is not to imply that Harmony for Hope has received every grant it has pursued.

It is to preserve the actual development history of the work.

That history includes:

```text
ideas
    ↓
concepts
    ↓
program development
    ↓
applications
    ↓
decisions
    ↓
lessons
    ↓
revisions
    ↓
future opportunities
```

That record matters because it shows how a complex community project becomes increasingly fundable, governable, and implementable over time.

The strongest grant portfolio is not one that hides unsuccessful or unfinished work.

It is one that makes clear **what was proposed, what was submitted, what was funded, what was learned, and what happens next**.
